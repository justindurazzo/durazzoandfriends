# What Do U.S.E.E.?

A walkthrough of the Kitchen Leadership professional development session by James Durazzo, with Chef Safaa. About 13 minutes.

Voices: NARRATOR sets the scene. JAMES presents. SAFAA assists. COOK and COOK 2 are example voices from the room.

---

## [00] Welcome

NARRATOR: Cooks arrive, sign in, and find a workbook and a pen at every seat. Breakfast is out. The coffee is hot.

JAMES: Good morning, everybody, and welcome. I'm James Durazzo, the Professional Development Manager for Staten Island with the Office of Food and Nutrition Services. And with me today is our District Cook Ambassador, Chef Safaa.

SAFAA: Good morning! I'm a cook, just like all of you. So I'm really glad to be here.

JAMES: Today's session comes from a framework we built for our new School Food Service Managers. We call it What Do U.S.E.E.? U.S.E.E. stands for Understanding, Safety, Efficiency, and Engagement. Four things every cook should look at before jumping in to fix whatever's happening in the kitchen. And I mean every cook. Whether you're in charge or you're an assistant cook.

JAMES: A few ground rules before we start. Have fun. Listen to understand. Share what you know, because every voice in here matters. Phones on vibrate, please. And no side conversations. If you've got something to say, say it to the whole room. We want to hear it.

## [01] The big question

JAMES: So here's the question for today. How can I use U.S.E.E. to lead my kitchen better, every single day?

JAMES: Every day, things come up that need a decision. U.S.E.E. gives you four ways to look at them. Do I understand what's actually going on? Is it safe? Is there a smarter way to do it? And how is this going to affect my team?

JAMES: By the end of today, it all comes down to one question you can carry with you. Before I act, what do U.S.E.E.?

## [02] Understanding

NARRATOR: Section one. Understanding.

JAMES: Understanding means knowing your team, your students, and your kitchen really well. But it's more than knowing what's happening. It's knowing what you can do about it.

JAMES: Because you can't spend your whole day frustrated by things you can't control. So let's sort it into three buckets. Control is what you own. Your tasks, your workflow, safety, coaching your people. Ask yourself, what can I do right now? Influence is what you can help improve, like morale and teamwork. And Communicate is what you need to pass up the line. Staffing, broken equipment, deliveries. Ask, who needs to know this so it gets fixed?

NARRATOR: The room splits into three groups. Each one gets a scenario and a stack of challenge cards to sort into three piles. Group one: someone called out, and lunch starts in forty five minutes. Group two: the milk chest breaks down in the middle of service. Group three: a hundred more students than usual just showed up for lunch.

NARRATOR: Ten minutes later, group two reports back.

COOK: Our milk chest broke during service. What we control: pull the milk, move it to another fridge, and temp it. What we influence: keep everybody calm and focused. What we communicate: report it to our manager and write down what happened.

JAMES: That's exactly right.

SAFAA: You know, that actually happened in my kitchen. And the very first thing I did was check the temperature of the milk. Because if it's already out of range, it doesn't matter where you move it.

JAMES: And that's the whole point. A lot of frustration at work doesn't come from bad intentions. It comes from acting on assumptions instead of facts. When we skip understanding, we solve the wrong problem, we miss information, we stress people out, and sometimes we put safety at risk.

JAMES: Understanding doesn't mean waiting. It means responding to the real problem, not just the first one you notice. Anyone have a time when a problem wasn't what it looked like?

SAFAA: I had a worker who was slow every single morning. I almost wrote him up. Then I asked him about it. Turns out the slicer was dull, and he was afraid to say anything. One question changed everything.

JAMES: Let's practice. Four scenarios. Someone's working really slowly during prep. The serving line is backing up and kids are waiting. The chicken is temping below holding temperature. And there's a wet floor by the dish machine. Which ones need action right now?

COOK: The chicken and the wet floor. That's safety. Somebody could get sick, or somebody could get hurt.

JAMES: That's right. Good leaders try to understand everything. But when safety's on the line, you act first.

## [03] Safety

NARRATOR: Section two. Safety.

JAMES: Understanding is where we start. But in a kitchen, sometimes you don't have time to think it over. When there's a hazard, safety comes first.

JAMES: Safety means protecting your staff, your students, and your food. And it shows up three ways. Physical safety: a clean kitchen, equipment used right, food handled right. Emotional safety: people talk to each other with respect, and they feel supported. And procedural safety: following the policies, the routines, and the regulations.

SAFAA: For physical safety, every morning before we start, I walk the floor. Cords, boxes, a wet spot. We fix it before anybody gets hurt.

COOK 2: For emotional safety, when somebody messes up, I don't yell in front of everybody. I pull them aside and we talk about it.

COOK: And procedural. Filling out the temp log every single day. Not on Friday for the whole week.

NARRATOR: Next, the safety scavenger hunt. Each group gets a big poster of a busy kitchen, and seven minutes to find every hazard they can.

COOK: Physical. A wet floor, and a guy running right through it carrying a stack of pans.

COOK 2: Procedural. The temp log is blank, and the allergen sign just says, ask your manager.

COOK: Emotional. The daily plan on the wall says hurry, do more with less, be perfect. And there's two people arguing in the corner.

JAMES: Look at how many there are. Now, what has to be fixed right away? And is there anything in this picture that's out of the Cook in Charge's control?

COOK 2: The broken equipment. We can't fix that ourselves. We report it.

JAMES: Exactly. That's the Understanding lens coming right back in. And here's the bigger idea. Don't just see the hazard. See why it's there. People are rushing. Stations aren't organized. Procedures get skipped. Nobody says anything. So you see the hazard, you see why it exists, and you fix the why. A safe kitchen is built by leaders who care, and teams who speak up.

## [04] Efficiency

NARRATOR: After a short coffee break, section three. Efficiency.

JAMES: We spent the morning finding hazards. But most safety problems don't start because people don't care. They start because something in the operation isn't working. Supplies aren't where they should be. People are rushing because the day wasn't planned.

JAMES: So, efficiency. And I want to be clear, efficiency is not working faster, and it's not doing more with fewer people. It's getting the best result with the least wasted time, effort, food, and energy, without giving up safety or quality.

JAMES: We look at it in four areas. Operational: is there an easier way to get the same result? Resources: are we wasting food, supplies, or energy? Time: what should happen first? And people: does everybody know what a good job looks like? These four are connected. Bad planning turns into time problems. Bad communication turns into people problems. Your job is to spot what's in the way, and move it.

SAFAA: I'll go first. For me, it's time. Our deliveries come right in the middle of prep, and everything stops. So on delivery days, we do our prep the afternoon before.

COOK 2: Mine's people. Nobody on my team is cross trained on the dish machine. So when one person's out, we're stuck. This month, I'm going to train two more people on it.

JAMES: That's it. Efficiency isn't more work. It's making it easier to do the right work.

## [05] Engagement

NARRATOR: Lunch. Somebody else cooks, for a change. Then section four, Engagement. And it starts with a game.

SAFAA: Okay, brain teaser. What has to be broken before you can use it?

COOK: An egg!

SAFAA: That's it! Next.

JAMES: Give yourselves a hand. And notice, what made that fun wasn't really the riddles. It was that everybody got to play.

JAMES: Engagement means building a kitchen where people feel valued, respected, and connected to why we're here, which is feeding kids. Kitchens don't succeed because of policies or schedules or equipment. They succeed because of people.

JAMES: So here's the activity. Write down one idea to get your staff, your students, or your school more involved in breakfast or lunch. Then, after each person shares, someone else is going to affirm them.

SAFAA: My idea is a monthly student taste test. The kids vote on a new recipe before it goes on the menu.

JAMES: Safaa, I love that. Kids eat what they help choose. It stands out to me because it gives students a real voice in our program. I can't wait to see how you put it in place, and if I can help, just let me know.

JAMES: That's engagement in action. People want to know their voice matters. Engagement is ownership. Stop saying, that's not my job. Start saying, how can I help us win? And it's purpose. People don't just follow instructions. They follow a reason. So tell them why.

JAMES: Two more tools. Recognition. Think about a manager who made you feel appreciated. What did they do?

SAFAA: My first manager thanked me by name, in front of the whole team, after a really hard day. I still remember it.

JAMES: Most of the time, appreciation isn't about awards. It's about being seen. Thank you is one of the most powerful things a leader can say. And inclusion. Some of the best ideas come from the people doing the work. So ask them. What do you think? What are you seeing? How would you fix it?

## [06] Putting it together

NARRATOR: The final section. Putting it all together.

JAMES: So that's all four. Understanding helps you make good decisions. Safety protects everyone. Efficiency clears the way. And engagement keeps people giving their best. Without engagement, the other three are hard to keep going.

JAMES: So next time you're facing a decision, run it through. Do I have the information? Is it safe? Is it the best use of our time, our resources, and our people? And will it bring my team closer together?

JAMES: Here's how this keeps going after today. Over the next thirty days, you bring U.S.E.E. into your kitchen. Within two weeks, you pick one SMART goal for your site, and your supervisor will come by to go over it with you. At thirty days, you'll check in on progress together. And I'll follow up with every one of you, too.

JAMES: SMART means Specific: what's it going to change, and why does it matter? Measurable: how will you know you did it? Achievable: can you do it with what you've got? Relevant: does it help us feed kids? And Time bound: when do you start, when do you finish, and when's your check in? Open your workbook to the SMART goal page, and let's write it now.

NARRATOR: For the next fifteen minutes, James and Chef Safaa walk the room, helping each cook make their goal specific to their own kitchen.

## [07] Close

JAMES: Thank you all for learning with us today. Your growth matters to us. Before you head out, please scan the QR code and fill out the quick survey. It really does help us get better.

SAFAA: Thank you, everyone. I'm one of you. So reach out anytime.

JAMES: And remember. Before you act, ask yourself. What do U.S.E.E.?
