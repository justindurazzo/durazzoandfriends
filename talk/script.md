# The Part That Can't Be Prompted

A talk by Justin Durazzo. About 12 minutes at a relaxed speaking pace (roughly 1,700 words).

Scene markers in brackets match the motion piece. Read the words, skip the brackets.

---

## [01] One arm (0:00)

In August I broke my arm.

Nothing dramatic. No good story. One second I was fine, and the next I was sitting in an urgent care with my arm in a sling, and a doctor telling me, very casually, six to eight weeks.

Six to eight weeks sounds short when somebody else says it.

What I didn't expect was how quickly the injury stopped being the problem. The arm, I could deal with. It hurt, and then it hurt less. The problem was everything around it.

## [02] A world built for two hands (0:36)

For the next few weeks I was a one-armed person living inside a world that was clearly designed for someone with two hands and a lot of patience.

Opening a jar. Cutting a steak. Putting on a jacket. Tying a shoe. Getting a phone out of your right pocket with your left hand while holding a coffee. You don't think about any of it until you can't do it.

And then there's the computer. Which I use, you know, all day. For work. Every tool I rely on assumes two hands on a keyboard. Shortcuts that need three fingers at once. Tiny buttons. Drag this into that. Hold shift and click. I'd sit down to answer one email and fifteen minutes later I'd still be on the first paragraph, hunting and pecking with my left hand like it was 1994.

I'll be honest, it made me feel a little stupid. And then it made me angry.

And I want to be careful here, because a broken arm is temporary. For me it was six weeks. For a lot of people, this is just Tuesday. Every day. Forever. The world isn't built for them either, and it never has been. I got a short, small taste of that, and it changed how I look at almost every screen I touch.

## [03] I started building (2:09)

But what surprised me wasn't the frustration. It was what happened next.

Because instead of waiting for someone to fix it, I just started building.

Not a company. Not a product. Little things. Fixes for my own life.

I set up voice dictation so I could write by talking instead of typing. And it turns out I'm better at saying what I mean than typing what I mean. The emails got shorter and clearer, which nobody complained about.

I built myself a simple way to track the recovery. Range of motion, pain, what I could do this week that I couldn't do last week. Because healing is slow enough that you can't feel it day to day. I wanted to actually see the progress I was feeling, so I'd stop thinking it wasn't happening.

I wired up my devices so I could move things around and get things done without touching anything. Lights, music, my calendar, my notes. Say it, and it happens.

None of it was hard, exactly. A year or two ago, most of it would have been a weekend project for a developer. This time it was me, one hand, talking to an AI, trying things, breaking things, trying again. Most of it was built in the gaps of a normal day.

And a lot of it didn't work at first. The dictation would hear "meeting" as "meat thing." The tracker had a bug where every day looked like the worst day. I'd fix one thing and break two others. But every fix took minutes, not weeks. And because I was the only user, I knew immediately when something was wrong. I didn't need a focus group. I just needed to try to open my email.

That loop, feel it, build it, try it, fix it, was faster than anything I'd ever done in my career.

## [04] Voice is the front door (4:20)

Somewhere in those weeks something became really obvious to me.

Voice is the front door to everything.

When you can't type, you find out how much of your life is locked behind a keyboard. And when you start talking to your tools instead, you realize the keyboard was never the point. It was just the way in. It was the way in because that's what we had.

I think the next real shift isn't a smarter chatbot. It's experiences that are led by the person, in their own words, the way they'd actually say it. Not menus. Not forms. Just, here's what I need, and the thing figures out the rest.

I didn't learn that from a trend report. I learned it because I needed it. I was the use case.

## [05] Everybody has the tool now (5:15)

Here's the part that I think matters for everyone in this room.

A study came out earlier this year that found about half of American adults are now using AI. Two years ago it was about a third.

That's fast. It's becoming as ordinary as owning a smartphone.

Which means me standing up here telling you to use AI is going to feel silly very soon. It would be like asking everyone here if they have a smartphone, and then walking you through how to open the camera.

So let's skip that part. Let's assume everybody has the tool.

Think about what happened with phones. When everyone got a camera in their pocket, it didn't make everyone a photographer. We took billions more pictures, and most of them are of lunch. But the people who were already looking closely at the world suddenly had a way to show us what they saw. The tool spread out. The seeing didn't.

If everybody has the tool, the tool isn't the difference.

So what is?

## [06] It isn't taste, either (6:28)

The answer you usually hear, especially from creative people, is taste. The machines can make things, but we have taste.

I don't think that's it. Or at least, I don't think it's the whole thing.

Taste has become the thing creative people say to reassure ourselves. It's comforting. It suggests we have some special sense that can't be copied.

But you could have pretty terrible taste and still be incredibly effective with these tools. I've seen it. And you could have impeccable taste and make nothing that matters to anybody.

Taste is also a little backward looking. It's knowing what good looked like before. That's useful. But the most interesting work usually doesn't look like what good looked like before. It looks a little wrong at first, until it doesn't.

So I don't think taste is the line.

## [07] Intention and repetition (7:26)

What I think matters more is intention, and repetition. And I think they sit on a sliding scale, the same way craft always has.

Sometimes a piece of work comes together in an afternoon. Because someone had something urgent to say and they said it clearly. You can feel that. It's alive. It doesn't need to be polished, because it's true.

And other times it's a thousand iterations. Draft after draft, the same problem from every angle, until it finally clicks. And you can feel that craft when you stand in front of it, too.

Both of those are real. What they share isn't taste. It's that someone cared what it meant and kept going until it meant that.

The tools are amazing at helping you do more iterations, faster. What they can't do is decide what you're trying to say.

## [08] When polish stops being proof (8:26)

The same is true for the work we do with clients.

We still have to find the real problem inside an organization. Not the problem in the brief. The real one. The thing people talk about in the hallway after the meeting. And we still have to find the real need in a culture, the thing people actually want, which is usually not what they ask for.

Here's what's changing. When everyone can make everything look finished, polish stops being proof of anything.

It used to be that a beautiful deck meant somebody spent a long time on it. The craft was evidence of the thinking. Not anymore. Now a deck can be beautifully organized, pitch perfect, every slide on brand, and have no heart at all. It's just a more sophisticated kind of slop.

So the question isn't, can we make it. Of course we can make it. We can make anything now.

The question is, should we? And what's the point of view?

I'll give you a small example. Years ago my brother and I and a few friends ran a pop-up grill at a dance party in Brooklyn, every Sunday, all summer. No roof, a big grill, a lot of smoke. Nobody came back week after week because the menu was well designed. They came back because we were obviously having the time of our lives, and we cared a lot about the food, and you could taste that. That's point of view. You can't fake it, and you can't generate it.

## [09] Felt experience (10:14)

For me, the point of view comes from felt experience.

A model can generate a thousand interfaces. It can write a thousand versions of this talk. But it has never been one-armed at seven in the morning, trying to answer a text with a coffee in the other hand, getting a little more frustrated with every typo.

It's never felt the problem.

I had to feel the problem first. That's what told me what to build. And then I could use the tools to build it, and iterate, and fix it, and make it better. The building part, the tools are great at. The feeling part is mine.

That's the part that can't be prompted.

## [10] What I'd leave you with (11:03)

So here's what I'd leave you with.

Everybody's going to have the same tools. That's already happening. Speed is going to be cheap. Polish is going to be cheap. Looking finished is going to be cheap.

What's not going to be cheap is knowing what matters, because you've lived it. Or because you went and talked to the people who have.

So pay attention to the moments when the world doesn't fit you. When something is harder than it should be. When you get annoyed. That's not just a bad day. That's information.

My arm's fine now, by the way. Mostly.

But I'm kind of grateful for those six weeks. They reminded me that the best work starts with a real problem, felt by a real person. And everything after that, we can build.

Thank you.
